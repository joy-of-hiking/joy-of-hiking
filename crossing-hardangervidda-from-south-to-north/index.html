Crossing Hardangervidda from South to North

October 27, 2025October 26, 2025 by hayaldeniz

In Late August 2022, we crossed Hardangervidda, a mountain plateau in central southern Norway. During our 11-day adventure, we wandered with our tents, saw almost no one but a bunch of lemmings and sheep, spontaneously joined a bridge-building group, and made our way from Haukeliseter, at the southern edge, to Finse, the northern end of the national park, just after passing by one of Norway’s largest glaciers, Hardangerjøkulen. This is the story of our hike, where I also share practical tips on hiking in Hardangervidda, including finding safe drinking water, camping in Norway, and using the DNT huts.

Getting to Haukeliseter, the starting point

To get to Norway from Germany, the most common option is to fly to Oslo and get a bus from there to the start of the trail. However, we wanted to make our traveling as eco-friendly as possible and had the time for a long journey. So, our two-day adventure began, with one train ride and two bus trips ahead of us. As there were direct buses from northern Germany to Oslo, we first took a train to Lübeck, made a half-day stop to visit some friends, and then continued our journey with a 14-hour night bus to Oslo.

Once arrived at the bus station in Oslo, we took another bus that brought us directly to the start of the trail in Haukeliseter, which took about 5 hours. Just after early afternoon, we arrived at the mountain lodge Haukeliseter. There, we picked up a key that gives access to all the mountain huts along the route, which we could return at the end of our hike in Finse. Although we planned to camp, having the key meant we could get supplies or a warm, dry place to sleep if needed.

Day 1 – Haukeliseter to Ålmannvegen, 3 km

As we were able to start the trail only after 5 pm, we decided to make a short day. After a steep 150-meter climb, we found a spot to pitch our tent with soft ground and not so far away from the river. We cooked the heaviest meal we had with ourselves to lighten our backpacks (instant spinach lentil curry) and made ourselves comfortable in our sleeping bags, ready to rest from the long trip and excited for the coming days.

In Norway, the Allemannsretten law (meaning “everyman’s right”) gives everyone the freedom to responsibly hike and camp on uncultivated land, from forests and mountains to coastal areas that make up most of the country’s wild nature. This freedom was one of our main motivations for hiking in Norway: we could stop whenever we were tired or found a spot we liked, without worrying about covering a certain number of kilometers each day to reach a hut or campground.

Day 2 – Ålmannvegen to Hellevatnet, 18 km 

On our second day, the rain clouds had disappeared, and a brilliant blue sky stretched above us as we hiked uphill to our first summit of the hike. The wilderness we faced made us already fall in love with the nature of Norway.

We crossed several streams, hopping from stone to stone and on wooden bridges. Along our way, we also had to cross several snowfields. This was our very first snowfield crossing, so we were so excited and proud once we were at the end of it. Hiking poles were definitely a big help, so do consider bringing a pair if you want to hike along this stretch as well.

This was also the day when we saw a lemming for the first time, which is a small, mouse-like animals that live in the Arctic. In Hardangervidda, every three to four years, lemmings reach peak population levels, which is called a lemming year. During this time, it’s not advisable to drink water directly from rivers without using a water filter or boiling it beforehand. The general rule of thumb is to only drink untreated water from sources when there are no animals nearby, though in some places that can be hard to determine. On the plateau, however, I found it quite easy, as the landscape was open and I could check the surroundings before filling my water bottle. When we were there, it wasn’t a lemming year. We didn’t use a filter but boiled the water when we were unsure, and we had no issues throughout the entire trip. That said, it’s wise to check ahead for a lemming year and plan accordingly.

Day 3 – Hellevatnet to Klerkatjørn, 15 km

On our third day, we had to cross more snowfields and some scree fields along the way, which, by this point, we were starting to get used to. Under the blue sky, we waved hello to the sheep we passed and practiced talking in their language, though we didn’t get much reply 🙁

Then, suddenly, Hårteigen finally appeared on the horizon, one of the highest peaks in Hardangervidda that would take us two days to reach. Hårteigen could be translated as “the gray guide”, referring to the fact that the mountain peak is visible from large parts of Hardangervidda and was important for travellers to find the direction, and still is.

After a steep hike downhill, we reached Klerkatjørn and found a nice camping spot to stay for the night. This region used to be home to a gang of robbers in the 17th century, and reading stories about them, we fell asleep.

Day 4 – Klerkatjørn to Hårteigen, 22 km

By the fourth day, I felt my daily struggles fade away, replaced by a deep calm, happiness, and gratitude for being able to witness this vast, beautiful nature. When you hike, life slows down to walking, eating, resting, and sleeping, which leaves plenty of time for groundbreaking experiments.

As our human-lemming counter reached 75 to 4, we reached Hårteigen and pitched our tent nearby. For dinner, we cooked vermicelli in instant mushroom soup with some soy granules for extra protein. 

Day 5 – Hårteigen to Hadlaskard, 16 km

After a tough night with a fever, which I hoped came from exhaustion and would soon go away, we decided to make a short day. After we took down the tent and packed our backpacks, we started heading towards the next hut, which was only 5 km away. From there, the vegetation slowly changed, and we saw the first tree of our hike. As I felt so much better, we kept going and headed towards the next hut in Hadlaskard, another 11 km.

We spent the night at Hadlaskard, a lovely little mountain cabin, which was perfect for resting and recharging for the days ahead. We met a few fellow hikers, enjoyed some rounds of kniffle, and then drifted off to sleep.

Day 6 & 7- Hadlarskard to Liseth, 28 km

After a good night’s rest at the mountain cabin, we were ready for the coming days. This part of Hardangervidda is noticeably greener; from time to time, the trail leads to narrow paths bordered by lush grass and birch forests. 

Once you arrive in Liseth, you can pitch your tent at the campground and pick up essentials at the small campsite shop.

Day 8 – Liseth to Rembesdalseter, 20 km

This day was the most challenging and also the most beautiful stretch of our hike, climbing around 1700 meters in altitude. After leaving Liseth behind, our first challenge was a long, steep climb past Smytte huts up to the 1200 m summit. From there, the second challenge awaited: a steep and tricky ascent that tested our legs and surefootedness. Even though it didn’t rain, the path was still slippery from nearby water sources.

Once the ascent was over, a steep descent began just a few kilometers later. But reaching the summit made it all worth it, with an incredible view of the Simadalsfjord. The path along the ridge was amazing.

Little did we know that only a few kilometers later, we would also be rewarded with a close-up view of a glacier tongue. Passing through it, the sudden cold was amazing.

Since there aren’t many water sources along the way, we decided to continue all the way to the Rembesdalseter cabin, only about 5 km ahead. The trail was filled with big rocks, which required some climbing from time to time, a challenge, but also super fun! Tired but happy, we finally made it to the cabin, where we could pitch our tent a short distance away.

Day 9 – Rembesdalseter Hut – Bridge building

The night before, we had met a group of volunteers who were rebuilding a wooden bridge nearby. We got along well and were asked if we wanted to join them for a day in exchange for staying the night in the cabin. We still had some days left and had zero intention of leaving the national park behind us, as we wanted to stay in nature as long as possible. So we decided to join the group and help them with the bridge construction. 

It was wonderful to stay in one place after spending each night somewhere different for the past week. It was also great to be part of the team, helping build something we had relied on as hikers the whole way. 

Apparently, the Norwegian Trekking Association (DNT) regularly organizes similar volunteer projects, where food, shelter, and a shared sense of purpose are offered to volunteers for their time and effort. The materials needed are delivered by the DNT, in our case, as we learned,  were flown in by helicopter, as the cabin would otherwise be inaccessible. 

Day 10 & 11 – Rembesdalseter to Finse, 20 km

On our next day, the freshly built bridge was still standing, and we had the honor of being among the first to cross it. But before we could cross the bridge, everyone had to sing an old Norwegian hiking song. I never found out which song it was, but it was a lovely moment.

After saying goodbye to everyone and to the bridge, we decided to take two days instead of one for the remaining 20 km to Finse. This stretch goes along one of Norway’s biggest glaciers, the and we decided to give ourselves more time to enjoy this vast beauty. 

And then, all of a sudden, we were back in civilization. The last stretch led us along Lake Finsevatnet for five kilometers, with views of the glacier giving us time to wave goodbye to the mountains. We arrived early at the DNT hut in Finse and enjoyed a beautiful rest day before heading back to Oslo the next day. We sat, played cards, and took a warm shower, our first since the hike had begun.

The next morning, we took the famous Oslo–Bergen train back to Oslo. After traveling the past two weeks only on foot, it felt like traveling at the speed of light. As the train passed through the mountains, we let the memories of the last two beautiful weeks sink in.
